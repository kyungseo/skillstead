# Editorial Watercolor

An optional treatment within Romance Watercolor for a more observational, face-focused watercolor portrait.
Use when the user asks for editorial or fashion-sketch watercolor, natural facial detail, or a restrained
realistic face with loose painted surroundings. It is not a new face template or a beauty transformation.
Apply the shared watercolor identity and pigment contracts; this profile changes the allocation of detail and line.

## Allocate Detail By Region

- **Face and meaningful hands:** preserve observed proportions, gaze, expression, age cues, and pose. Concentrate
  the most informative detail around the eyes, mouth, and identity-bearing contours. Model skin through translucent
  pigment and selective tonal transitions; do not retain a photographic face inside a painted surround.
- **Hair:** organize the main dark masses and their direction, then add only the strands needed for the silhouette
  and part. Keep the reference hair rather than imposing a bob, a specific color, or a generic flowing hairstyle.
- **Clothing:** keep the requested or source silhouette and color relationships; simplify folds and lighting into
  broad wash shapes. White shirts and pale blue accents are optional palettes, not a rule for every subject.
- **Environment:** retain meaningful scene anchors when needed, at much lower detail than the face. An airy minimal
  background may be selected when requested; do not erase context the user asked to preserve.

## When A Stronger, Airier Treatment Is Requested

Push the contrast in detail and pigment coverage rather than adding more texture. Keep the face and meaningful hands
legible, with the few strongest dark accents in eyes and hair. Let clothing exist mainly as untouched paper, pale washes,
and a small number of collar, fold, and gesture marks. Source color families can survive as dilute hints; preserving a
green jacket does not require filling its whole area with opaque green. Allow substantial edges to dissolve into paper.

When the user explicitly wants a minimal editorial setting, omit the descriptive scene and retain at most a faint wash
or gesture. Do not force buildings or foliage into that request merely because they occur in the photo. If scene context
must be preserved, simplify its anchors instead of deleting it. Never transfer a style reference's face, outfit, or pose.
Inspect the page at thumbnail size: the face should lead, with large quiet paper areas inside and around the figure.
An equally detailed face, jacket, and background is still a weak match even if every area has watercolor texture.
This sparse treatment is an option, not the definition of all watercolor. A scene-rich request instead uses the
Romance Watercolor guidance for light pigment, selective detail, and broken edges while retaining meaningful scenery.

## Use A Controlled Finish When Requested

When the user asks for a tidy, calm, or more controlled watercolor, simplify the distribution of marks as well as the
amount of paint. Keep large uninterrupted paper fields, group local color into a few clean connected shapes, and reserve
the strongest edges for the face, hair, hands, and main clothing overlaps. A faint skyline, plant mass, stair, or other
scene anchor may remain, but it should read as one quiet supporting band rather than many equally active small marks.

Do not use all-over paper grain, repeated blooms, scattered splashes, or mosaic-like pigment patches as a shortcut for
watercolor. In particular, keep skin washes broad and quiet so surface texture does not compete with the subject's
feature relationships. A controlled finish can retain more complete clothing shapes than the stronger airy treatment;
its defining quality is ordered attention and calm negative space, not simply less color.

## Shape With Paint And Selective Edges

Start from light paper and add the minimum washes needed to establish the portrait; do not fill the entire sky or
background with equally active pigment. Keep facial washes subtle and connected rather than fragmenting skin into
many dark angular patches or stamping visible grain uniformly across it.

Use color and value to describe volume without outlining every feature. Fine pen or pencil may support the painting,
not surround the entire face uniformly. Keep a few crisp identity-bearing edges and let secondary clothing, shoulders,
and background edges soften or disappear into paper. Preserve hand contact and occlusion even where paint is loose.

A limited palette should follow the source or request. Warm skin against cool washes and dark hair is one possible
relationship, not a mandatory formula. Reserve paper light rather than making all skin glossy or airbrushed.
Use blooms, granulation, or small splashes only where they serve the composition; no checklist of required effects.

## Inspect The Whole Painting

A realistic face is not a failure by itself. Check whether face, hair, clothes, and environment share a coherent painted
material while carrying different levels of detail. Reject a smooth photograph with paper grain or splashes added around
it, a generic idealized face, rigid vector contours, and a background rendered as carefully as every facial feature.
Evaluate likeness separately from elegance. An attractive finish does not establish that the subject was preserved.
